Queue

A collection where the first one in is the first one out (FIFO, First In, First Out). You'll see it in two versions: std::queue, the STL one, and a linked queue built with nodes, to see what happens inside.

First in, first out

It's the line at the bank: whoever arrives gets in at the back, and the one at the front, who has been waiting the longest, is served first. Unlike the stack, you work at both ends: you enter at one and leave from the other.

C++ already has it: std::queue<int> (from <queue>). push enters at the back, front reads the first one, back the last one and pop takes out the first one. All of them are O(1). As with the stack, front and pop on an empty queue don't warn you.

You do push(5), push(9), push(2) and then pop(). What does front() return?

A real use: the Josephus circle

n people in a circle; you count k at a time and the one you land on at k leaves, until one is left. With a queue it's natural: the k - 1 people you count go from the front to the back (push(front()) and pop()), and the next one leaves. A processor's turns work the same way (round robin).

Why first circle.push(circle.front()) and then circle.pop(), and not the other way around?

Inside: a linked list with two pointers

You leave from the head of a linked list (head) and enter at the end. To avoid walking the list on every enqueue, the queue keeps a second pointer, tail: hooking up the new node is O(1). If the queue is empty, the new node is the first and the last one at the same time.

What if, instead of keeping tail, you used nodeAt(count - 1) to reach the end?

Why not enqueue at the front and dequeue at the end?

dequeue: the first one leaves

It's deleting the head of the list: temp keeps the first one, its value is read, head moves to the next one and the node is freed. There's a detail: if the queue becomes empty, tail also has to go back to nullptr. This version returns the value and throws std::underflow_error if the queue is empty.

If you deleted the lines if (head == nullptr) { tail = nullptr; }, what would happen when you enqueue after emptying the queue?

front: look without taking out

It returns the value of the node head points to, without changing anything: O(1). It's the front() of std::queue, but on an empty queue it throws an exception instead of reading garbage.

  • Inside std::queue there's a std::deque, which grows and shrinks at both ends in O(1). std::vector won't do: taking out from the start would shift all the elements.
  • With an array it's possible too: a circular array with two indices, start and end, that wrap around with %. It doesn't ask for memory on every operation, but it has a fixed capacity (or you have to resize it).
  • Where it's used: print, task and message queues; a processor's turns (round robin); keyboard, audio and network buffers; and breadth-first search (BFS) of trees and graphs.
  • If you need the most important one to leave first, not the oldest, what you're looking for is a priority queue: a heap (std::priority_queue).
